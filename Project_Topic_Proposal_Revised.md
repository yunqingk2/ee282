# EE282 Project Topic Proposal: Chromatin Accessibility Dynamics During Early *Xenopus tropicalis* Development

## Introduction

Early embryonic development requires a precisely coordinated transition: a shift from being regulated by maternal factors to being driven by transcription from the embryo’s own genome (Gentsch et al. 2019). This process is known as zygotic genome activation (ZGA) and involves changes in chromatin accessibility that help determine which regulatory DNA sequences are available to transcription factors (Gentsch et al. 2019; Cui et al. 2025). Understanding these changes is important to explain how developmental gene regulation begins. For my final project, I propose to analyze publicly available CANTAC-seq data from early *Xenopus tropicalis* embryos to characterize developmental patterns of chromatin accessibility and identify transcription factor motifs associated with those patterns. My motivation is to gain practical experience in ATAC-seq analysis for future research in computational genomics and gene regulation. ATAC-seq uses transposase-mediated sequencing to profile accessible chromatin (Buenrostro et al. 2013). 

CANTAC-seq was developed to improve the measurement of accessible chromatin in early frog embryos. It uses concanavalin A beads to capture nuclei before Tn5-mediated profiling of accessible DNA (Cui et al. 2025). Cui et al. (2025) used this method to follow chromatin accessibility during early development and reported a substantial increase in accessible regions during ZGA. They also combined motif analysis with perturbation experiments to study the roles of E2f1 and Otx1. My project will focus on the wild-type developmental time course. The published results will provide a reference for my analysis, but my goal is to learn how to process and interpret accessibility data rather than reproduce all of the study’s experiments. In particular, I am interested in whether regions with similar changes in accessibility also share sequence motifs.

## Data

I will use the wild-type *X. tropicalis* CANTAC-seq samples deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession [GSE232071](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE232071) (Cui et al. 2025). The dataset includes stages 7, 8, and 9, corresponding to early, middle, and late blastula; stage 10, the onset of gastrulation; and stage 13, the onset of neurulation. Each stage has two independent biological replicates (Cui et al. 2025). This gives a total of ten samples for comparing accessibility across development and checking consistency within stages. I will also use the *X. tropicalis* version 10.0 reference genome and compatible Xenbase gene annotations.

## Research Questions and Exploratory Analysis

The first question is how the number and distribution of accessible regions change across development. I will begin by checking read quality, trimming adapters, aligning reads with Bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012), and processing the alignments with SAMtools (Li et al. 2009). I will assess mapping quality, duplication, fragment-size distributions, and the fraction of reads in peaks. MACS2 will be used to identify accessible regions (Zhang et al. 2008; Cui et al. 2025), and agreement between biological replicates will help determine which peaks are reproducible. I will then use BEDTools to compare peak locations across stages and annotate their positions relative to genes (Quinlan and Hall 2010). Summaries of peak counts and overlap will show which regions are detected at several stages and which are first detected later. These comparisons will also help identify differences that need closer inspection before biological interpretation. 

The second question is whether accessible regions can be grouped by their developmental patterns. Some regions may be accessible early, whereas others may show increasing signal or become accessible only at later stages. I will combine reproducible peaks across stages into a common set of regions and measure normalized signal at the same coordinates in every sample. This will allow me to examine signal at a region even when it does not meet the peak-calling threshold in every stage. I will use deepTools to prepare coverage summaries and signal matrices (Ramírez et al. 2016), then use R for summary statistics and visualization. Replicate correlations will help assess consistency before I summarize stage-level patterns.

Heatmaps, average signal profiles, and exploratory k-means clustering will be central to this comparison. Heatmaps will show how individual regions vary across stages, while average profiles will make the patterns within groups easier to compare. These analyses connect the project to the course’s emphasis on exploratory data analysis and visualization: I will use plots to inspect variation, assess data quality, and decide which patterns warrant further analysis. I will interpret differences cautiously because sequencing depth and peak-calling thresholds can affect the apparent number of accessible regions. Normalized coverage will describe relative accessibility rather than provide an absolute measure of genome-wide chromatin opening.

The third question is whether regions with different accessibility patterns are enriched for different transcription factor motifs. I will use HOMER to analyze selected groups with clear and reproducible patterns (Heinz et al. 2010). Where enough regions are available, I will also examine regions first detected at stages 8, 9, 10, and 13, following the paper’s stage-based comparisons. I will separate promoter-proximal and distal regions using the study’s definition: proximal regions overlap the interval from 1,000 bases upstream to 100 bases downstream of an annotated transcription start site (Cui et al. 2025). I will use background sequences chosen to account for sequence length and GC content (Heinz et al. 2010). This comparison will help distinguish motifs associated with promoters from those enriched in more distant candidate regulatory regions. Enriched motifs will suggest candidate transcription factor families; they will not demonstrate that a specific protein binds those regions.

## Conclusions

This project is feasible because the sequencing data and reference resources are publicly available, the analysis uses established tools, and no new experiments are required. Access to the university’s HPC3 cluster will support alignment and other computationally demanding steps. I will work through quality assessment, peak comparisons, visualization, and a limited set of motif analyses, documenting the workflow in my ee282 GitHub repository. If processing all five stages takes too long, I will prioritize stages 7, 8, and 10 to retain a comparison across early development and the onset of gastrulation. Even if motif enrichment does not yield a clear result, the quality summaries and accessibility plots will address the main exploratory questions. This scope will allow me to complete an informative course project while gaining experience in ATAC-seq analysis for future research.
